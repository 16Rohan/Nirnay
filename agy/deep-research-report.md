# NIRNAY Wargaming Research Synthesis

## Executive Summary  
Wargaming and military simulation are broad practices used to test strategies and decisions without actual combat.  In India, however, detailed public documentation of structured wargames is scarce.  The Indian Army conducts large-scale field and command-post exercises (e.g. Shoorveer, Rudra Akrosh, Shatrujeet) that include “war games” phases to validate tactics and readiness.  For example, in May 2012 a series of Indian “war games” aimed to validate the “operational and transformational effectiveness” of a Western Army Command’s formations.  Beyond these exercises, most information on Indian methodology comes from announced programs.  Notably, Project WARDEC (an AI-powered virtual wargame center) will use VR/AR environments, terrain and weather modeling, and AI-generated adversaries.  Outside India, established doctrine emphasizes realistic scenarios, “fog of war” (uncertainty) and information asymmetry, after-action reviews of outcomes, and a mixture of deterministic/stochastic adjudication.  This synthesis collates available evidence and open-source principles; where Indian sources are lacking, it draws on general wargaming literature and allied practice, with citations to published material.

## Indian Wargaming  
There is little official public detail on India’s internal wargaming techniques.  Indian defense exercises are mostly field or command-post drills.  For example, **Exercise Shoorveer (2012)** was a massive desert maneuver involving 50,000 troops in live tactical and combined-arms training.  **Exercise Rudra Akrosh (2012)** explicitly included “several war games” to test operational concepts: its report notes the war games were aimed at validating “the operational and transformational effectiveness of various formations”.  Similarly, **Exercise Shatrujeet (2016)** tested integrated air–land strike capabilities.  These examples show India uses *play-of-force* wargaming within larger exercises to evaluate plans.  However, details on scenario setup, participant roles, turns, adjudication and structured lessons capture are not publicly documented. Indian sources do not specify, for instance, whether fixed “Red” and “Blue” teams are used in exercises or how outcomes are scored.  It appears that Indian wargaming largely follows conventional models (e.g. tabletop planning followed by simulation or field trials), but we did not find open documentation of formal wargame formats or debrief methods.  

## Indian Red Teaming  
Similarly, there is no publicly available doctrine on dedicated Red Teams in India.  In Western practice, “Red teams” play adversaries to challenge assumptions; their absence in Indian open sources suggests any red-teaming is informal or subsumed under exercise planning.  Indian publications do mention counter-terror and insurgency drills, but no source describes a formal Red-Team process.  In effect, how India “challenges assumptions” in planning is not described in open literature.  General practice (by analogy) would use dedicated planners or opposition forces to generate alternative courses of action and inject surprises.  Explicitly, we found **no Indian doctrinal reference** to red-team roles or procedures. (Outside India, by contrast, militaries routinely set up Red/Blue/White cells in exercises, but no India-specific reference was found.)  

## Indian Technology-Based Wargaming  
Indian defense is explicitly moving toward tech-enabled simulation.  The most concrete public program is **Project WARDEC** (Wargame R&D Centre), announced by the Army and Rashtriya Raksha University.  WARDEC is envisaged as “India’s first simulation-based training centre” using **AI to design virtual reality wargames**.  It will provide a metaverse-like VR/AR environment for officers to train on full-scenario games.  Reportedly, RRU’s IT and AI school (with Tech Mahindra) will build the simulator; variables like terrain slope, weather, time of day, air pressure, surveillance reach, artillery range, troop positions, and even soldier health and enemy reaction will be modeled by the AI.  For example, one description notes that in the simulation “enemy can ambush you from 361 degrees,” forcing consideration of all sectors.  These features indicate WARDEC will be an **integrated combat simulation with high realism** (terrain, C3 networks, weapon effects).  Other public references to Indian tech wargaming are scarce.  Various Indian forces likely use basic simulators (e.g. flight sims, vehicle simulators) and possibly tabletop digital tools, but no details were found beyond WARDEC.  The general trend noted in media is that post-9/11, major militaries (US, UK, Israel, etc.) have adopted IT-enabled wargaming, and India’s WARDEC aligns with that move.  

## Joint and Multi-Domain Considerations  
India’s defense doctrine increasingly emphasizes jointness and multi-domain integration, but open sources say little about how this affects wargaming.  Public doctrinal documents (e.g. “Joint Doctrine” or tri-service war games) are not easily found.  The exercises above (Shoorveer, Shatrujeet) imply combined arms (land–air) and nuclear-biological-chemical (NBC) contingencies.  Indian exercises with other countries (e.g. Shakti with France) include peacekeeping under UN Charter, hinting at multi-domain planning.  However, we found **no specific model** for including cyber, space or information domains in Indian wargames.  In general, credible multi-domain wargaming would account for contributions of land, air, maritime, space, cyber and cognitive operations.  In the absence of Indian references, one notes that Western “multi-domain operations” concepts (e.g. JADC2) integrate sensors and shooters across domains; India’s versions (e.g. networks for cross-service C4ISR) might similarly be injected into exercises.  No source explicitly documents such scenarios.  In summary, joint land–air–naval themes appear in Indian exercises, but structured multi-domain or cyber/information scenarios have not been described publicly for Indian games.

## Wargame Formats  
Wargames take many formats.  While specific Indian formats are undocumented, the general categories (from international practice) include:  
- **Tabletop Exercises (TTX)** – Low-tech staff game where planners (usually HQ staff and observers) discuss a scenario on map boards. Purpose: test COAs and plans rapidly. Participants: commanders, staff officers (often small). Inputs: scenario briefs, maps, counters. Iteration: Sequential decision “turns” in discussion. Adjudication: Facilitated by a controller; outcomes determined by rules or umpire. Strength: quick and flexible for concept exploration. Limitations: subjective outcomes, depends on participants’ expertise. Use cases: early design of plans, staff training.  
- **Command Post Exercises (CPX)** – Headquarters-centric exercise where command elements execute a scenario using simulation tools. Purpose: train staff procedures and C2 processes. Participants: unit commanders and staff in command HQ. Inputs: detailed scenario injects, digital maps or sand tables. Iteration: Real-time role-play, often several days’ worth of operations. Adjudication: Often aided by a digital simulation (e.g. ISTAR feeds); umpires may make calls. Strength: realistic tempo and communications. Limitations: no live troops (only virtual losses), expensive.  
- **Computer-Assisted (CAX)** – Digital war game using professional simulation software (e.g. JCATS, LAS, JTLS). Purpose: train and analyze at brigade/div level. Participants: operators and umpires, often with remote participation. Iteration: Turn or near real-time, with computer resolving combat outcomes per programmed models. Adjudication: Automated physics-based or probabilistic models handle attrition; humans may inject events. Strength: detail, replayability. Limitations: model fidelity, assumptions transparency.  
- **Constructive Simulation** – Fully computerized exercise where both Blue and Red are played by simulation or AI. Humans plan and review rather than move pieces. Often used for campaign-level analysis or staff college war games. Inputs: scenario and order of battle data. Adjudication: All outcomes via simulation algorithms. Strength: scale and variability. Limitation: little human role-play, heavy reliance on model.  
- **Field Training Exercises (FTX)** – Live drills with actual units. Though not “games,” these serve to test plans under a war-game-like scenario with real arms (for example, tanks and planes). Adjudication: Live feedback and umpire reports.  
- **Red vs Blue (Opposition)** – Many exercises use opposing forces. In Indian use, e.g. joint army-air force exercises would inherently have a blue side (friendly) and script an enemy (red) or use role-players. Branch-and-sequel formats (where exercises split into alternate futures) are common in Western practice, but no example was found for India specifically.  
- **After-Action Reviews** – Regardless of format, exercises typically end with an AAR.  An AAR is a facilitated session comparing intended vs actual outcomes to draw lessons. It is forward-looking (to improve next time) and avoids blame, a practice formalized by the US Army. We presume Indian exercises similarly conduct debriefs, although no source explicitly describes the Indian AAR process.  

## Scenario Construction & Design Principles  
Professional wargames share design principles to ensure useful outcomes. Key elements include setting clear **objectives and constraints**, establishing a **scenario narrative** with context and initial conditions, and defining **adversarial courses of action (COAs)**.  Wargames must include **friction/fog of war** – uncertainty and randomness – to model real conditions.  For example, variables like limited information (e.g. hidden enemy units) create “fog” so that players do not know all adversary positions.  Scenario design often identifies critical decision points or events (e.g. a weather shift, arrival of reinforcements) to test adaptability.  **Resource/time pressures** are built in (e.g. deadlines for decisions, resupply limits).  Plans usually assume the enemy will adapt; many games allow the Red side to change strategy in response to Blue moves.  In summary, methodologies emphasize realism, uncertainty, and learning: participants formulate intent, execute, and then review differences between expected vs actual outcomes.  While these principles are documented internationally, we found no India-specific doctrine; thus we extrapolate from common practice (see Comparative Findings below).

## Environment Modeling  
Wargames model the battlefield environment because terrain and conditions critically affect outcomes.  Key variables in professional wargames include terrain and elevation (hills, cover), infrastructure (roads, bridges), weather (precipitation, wind, visibility), light/time (day/night cycles), and mobility (off-road capability).  Communications and logistics networks are also factors.  From our sources, **India’s WARDEC simulation will explicitly include many of these:** virtual terrain and slope, weather, atmospheric pressure, artillery range, surveillance reach, and friendly/enemy positions are “all taken into account” by the AI.  This suggests Indian models will consider geography and climate as important.  In other militaries, digital terrain models (DTMs) and Geographic Information Systems (GIS) are routinely used to generate simulated maps, and weather can be simulated by injecting random patterns or historical data.  Indian sources did not name specific environment models, but emphasize that **terrain and weather materially affect outcomes** – for instance, validating a strike corps’ deep attack capability would consider desert terrain and possible chemical contamination.  In short, credible wargames vary terrain and weather to challenge players; WARDEC’s planned features highlight Indian planners’ intent to do so (e.g. modeling slopes and visibility).

## Adjudication and Simulation  
The core of any wargame is how combat outcomes are decided (“adjudication”).  Approaches range from manual rules or judgment to automated simulation.  Common methods include:  
- **Deterministic simulation** – Fixed results given inputs (rare for combat unless simplified).  
- **Probabilistic/stochastic models** – Use random draws based on force ratios and weapon effectiveness. The game software or dice rolls determine outcomes.  
- **Rule-based adjudication** – War game umpire applies outcome tables or rules of engagement.  
- **Computer/agent-based simulation** – Software algorithms (often physics-based) resolve combat. Many modern exercises use computer models to tally losses, detect fields of fire, etc.  
- **Human adjudication** – In low-tech games, umpires (experts) decide what happens.  
- **Hybrid systems** – Combine computer models with human overrides.  

Our research question asked what *should* be left to simulation vs. to a language model.  Current credible practice is to let a specialized simulation engine handle kinematics and known physical interactions (e.g. trajectories, detection), often with stochastic elements.  AI or LLM approaches could generate scenario text, dialogues, and strategic suggestions, but routine battle resolution is normally model-driven.  No source directly addresses language models, but by analogy, Indian practice (and that of NATO/US) would likely have AI/comp-assisted guns and sensors simulated deterministically, while human-in-the-loop makes decisions.  Importantly, any adjudication must preserve **uncertainty**: a combat result may not be fully predictable to players beforehand.  Thus, even with AI tools, retention of randomness (dice or Monte Carlo) is “documented methodology” globally, though no Indian-specific citation was found on this split of roles.  

## Scenario Diversity and Branching  
Professional games avoid repetitive outcomes by varying initial conditions and injecting randomness.  Common techniques (though not specifically documented for India) include:  
- **Multiple or randomized starting states:** E.g. enemy dispositions or resources differ each run.  
- **Branch-and-sequel structure:** If play splits into alternate futures, different follow-on exercises explore each branch. (Used by NATO for crisis simulations.)  
- **Dynamic red adaptation:** The opposition force modifies its plan in response to Blue, leading to different paths.  
- **Stochastic elements:** Random weather changes, fog of war (e.g. sensor failures), or surprise events (e.g. third-party actions) ensure variability.  
- **Decision point variation:** Changing the timing or options at key junctures (e.g. reinforcements arrive early or late).  

These methods aim to produce plausible but not predetermined results.  The key is to keep games grounded in logic (e.g. a random flood is less useful than simulating a known rainy season event).  No Indian source was found on technique, but Project WARDEC’s design (with AI-driven scenario generation) implies India values automated variety.  A quote about “361-degree ambush” suggests intent to force consideration of unexpected approaches.  Altogether, credible methodology discourages fixed outcomes by layering uncertainty and letting Red change course.  

## Emergent Events and Injects  
Wargames often include “injects” – unexpected events to test adaptability.  Typical injects might simulate political shifts, allied negotiations, equipment breakdowns, or intelligence coups.  For instance, a game might have a ceasefire suddenly ordered, or fake news alter an assumption.  We found no Indian examples, but general practice is to script injects in advance or allow umpires to improvise.  For example, Indian exercises might simulate a monsoon flood or sudden border incident.  The Project WARDEC concept (metaverse simulation) even suggests dynamic environmental changes (e.g. “if a weapon drops or air pressure falls, they will feel it”). This implies that unexpected changes (air pressure, VR feedback) are considered.  In summary, credible wargames build in unpredictable developments; if the Indian Army plans similarly, they likely use manually added events or AI triggers, but specifics are not documented.

## Comparative Practices  
Outside India, NATO and allied militaries have formal wargaming doctrines.  For example, Western militaries often follow a design process of **mission/objectives, assumptions, COA analysis, wargaming, assessment, and AAR**.  Doctrines emphasize realism and learning.  US/UK/NATO exercises use a mix of **tabletop, constructive, and virtual simulations**, with clear Red/Blue/White roles.  The US Army for instance runs numerous brigade/regimental wargames (often computer-assisted) to prepare commanders.  Academic and RAND studies note that enabling information ambiguity, exercise injects, and robust AAR capture lessons are universal best practices.  None of our Indian sources contradict these general rules; indeed, the Indian press description of WARDEC closely mirrors global trends in AI/VR wargaming.  The Fog of War concept and formal AAR practice come from Western theory but are plainly relevant.  Transferable principles include: design for uncertainty, ensure defined evaluation metrics, and incorporate human judgment.  Specific methods (e.g. NATO’s branch-and-sequel SOP, or US Marine Red Teaming doctrine) may be worth consultation but are not detailed in Indian sources.  In sum, comparative evidence suggests India is moving in line with global best practices (AI/VR use, uncertainty modeling), but official Indian methodology details remain opaque.

## Areas of Uncertainty and Research Gaps  
The publicly available evidence is incomplete.  **Key gaps** include:
- **Indian Wargaming Methodology:** No public manual or doctrinal source on Indian wargame structure, rules, or decision procedures. We do not know how turns are managed, how black/white forces are organized beyond general exercise descriptions, or how lessons are recorded in Indian practice.  
- **Red Teaming:** No official descriptions of formal Red Teams or how adversarial options are generated internally.  
- **Joint/Multi-domain Integration:** Lack of documentation on cyber, space, or joint-intelligence injection into wargames. Doctrinal focus (publicly) is on conventional domains.  
- **Adjudication Mechanics:** No insight into any Indian combat resolution models or if they rely on human judgment vs. computer.  
- **Variety Mechanisms:** Apart from WARDEC hints, we found no Indian guidelines for branching, scenario variability, or random inject rules.  

These gaps likely reflect either classification of such details or simply an absence of published doctrine.  Inferred from practice, India appears to borrow widely from allied thinking (e.g. using AARs, VR-simulation), but the research did not uncover Indian-specific “methods papers.”  Therefore, confidence in details is low; many design assumptions must come from general wargaming expertise rather than India-specific evidence.

## Design-Relevant Insights  
Based on credible methodology, the following findings are most relevant for NIRNAY (without prescribing implementation):  

- **Use Scalable Formats:** Table-top staff games, CPXs, and full simulations each serve roles. For high-level strategy, flexible tabletop brainstorming is common; for operational training, computer-assisted CPXs provide detail. NIRNAY could support multiple formats, as others do.  
- **Emphasize Uncertainty:** Incorporate fog-of-war and limited info to force realistic decision-making. The Indian emphasis on “everywhere ambush” suggests NIRNAY should model wide situational uncertainty (e.g. unseen threats, environment changes).  
- **After-Action Review:** A structured debrief comparing intent vs result is essential. NIRNAY should ensure logging of decisions and outcomes to enable AAR analysis. This is a nearly universal best practice.  
- **Environment Factors:** Since WARDEC will simulate terrain, weather, etc, NIRNAY design should similarly model key environmental effects (mobility over different terrain, visibility limits).  
- **Red-Blue Dynamics:** Even if formal Red Teams are undocumented in India, opposition play is central. NIRNAY should allow for dynamic adversary plans and injection of unexpected enemy actions.  
- **AI/VR Technologies:** The trend toward AI-generated scenarios and virtual training indicates Indian appetite for tech-enabled wargaming. NIRNAY can leverage AI for scenario generation and provide immersive (VR/AR) interfaces as envisioned by WARDEC.  
- **Branching Scenarios:** Credible exercises use branching/sequels and randomness to explore alternatives. NIRNAY should support split scenarios or replayability to produce varied outcomes without being arbitrary.  

These insights align with “credible wargaming methodology” as documented elsewhere.  They should be considered as design guidance – e.g. NIRNAY might implement uncertainty and after-action analysis – but are not direct implementation requirements from research sources.
